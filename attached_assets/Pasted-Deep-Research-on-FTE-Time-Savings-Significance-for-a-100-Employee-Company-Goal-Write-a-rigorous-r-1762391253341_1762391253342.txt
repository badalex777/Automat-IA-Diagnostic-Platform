Deep Research on FTE Time-Savings Significance (for a 100-Employee Company)

Goal
Write a rigorous research paper that determines how many full-time equivalents (FTEs) in time savings should be considered “100% business impact” on a 0–100 scale for a company with 100 employees. Also quantify what 1 FTE saved represents on that same 0–100 scale. Use external evidence to justify the thresholds.

Deliverables (2000–3000 words, with tables, figures, and citations)

Executive Summary (key findings, recommended threshold for 100% impact, what 1 FTE equals on the 0–100 scale)

Definitions & Assumptions

Define FTE; state typical annual hours (e.g., 2,080) and discuss regional variations.

Define “time savings,” “FTE-equivalent,” “productivity gain,” and “capacity redeployment vs. cost reduction.”

Literature Review (with citations)

Evidence on typical automation/productivity gains and FTE reductions in SMEs and mid-market firms.

Thresholds where savings move from incremental to transformative (e.g., effects on margins, cycle time, service levels, or the ability to avoid hiring).

Quantitative Modeling for a 100-Employee Company

Compute how FTE savings translate to % of workforce capacity: %CapacitySaved = (FTE_Saved / 100) × 100%.

Test candidate “100% impact” thresholds (e.g., 5, 8, 10, 12, 15 FTEs) and justify which is most defensible based on evidence.

Distinguish two scenarios:
a) Pure cost take-out (headcount avoidance or external spend reduction).
b) Capacity redeployment (time reallocated to higher-value work).

Include sensitivity to: utilization (e.g., 70–90%), operating margin bands, salary mix, and variability in annual hours (1,800–2,200).

Scoring Model (clear, auditable)

Propose a mapping from FTE saved → Impact Score (0–100) for a 100-employee company. Provide at least two forms and defend one:
• Linear: Impact = min(100, (FTE_Saved / FTE_100%_Threshold) × 100)
• Piecewise / S-curve to capture diminishing or compounding returns; specify breakpoints and rationale.

Explicitly state the recommended FTE_100%_Threshold and compute what 1 FTE equals on the 0–100 scale (e.g., if threshold = 10 FTE, then 1 FTE = 10 points).

Impact Tables & Charts

Table: FTE saved from 1 to 20 → Impact Score (0–100) under each candidate threshold.

Chart: The chosen scoring curve vs. alternatives (linear vs. S-curve).

Table: Sensitivity results (how the recommended threshold shifts with utilization, margin, wage mix).

Practical Interpretation

When would smaller FTE savings still merit a 100 score (e.g., if the savings bottleneck a critical function)? Provide criteria and examples.

How to adapt the threshold for different company sizes (e.g., 50, 200 employees).

Limitations

Note data gaps, sector differences, and any assumptions that meaningfully affect the threshold.

References

Use numbered citations to reputable sources (academic, government, industry). Provide a bibliography.

Method Requirements

Use real benchmarks from studies on automation, productivity, and labor reallocation; cite all figures.

Provide at least three candidate thresholds for “100% impact,” evaluate them against evidence, and choose one.

Show the math for all formulas. Include a reproducible example with concrete numbers (e.g., wage mix, utilization).

Be neutral and evidence-driven; avoid vendor or tool marketing.

Key Questions to Answer Explicitly

For a 100-employee company, what FTE savings level is the most defensible boundary for calling the impact “transformative” (i.e., a 100 score)?

Given that boundary, what score does 1 FTE saved represent?

Under what conditions (e.g., role criticality, bottlenecks, high external spend) should the threshold be adjusted up or down?

How do cost-reduction vs. capacity-redeployment pathways change interpretation of the score?

Output Format

Professional research report with numbered sections, tables, figures, and a numbered bibliography.

Provide the scoring formula(s) in a boxed “Model Specification” section and a one-page appendix with the full parameter list so the scoring can be implemented elsewhere.
